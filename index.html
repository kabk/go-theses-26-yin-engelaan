Introduction

Command Z is one of the most natural gestures in my design practice. With a single shortcut, decisions can be reversed, adjusted, and refined. Typography shifts by fractions of a millimetre, layouts are endlessly rearranged, and variations are without consequence. In this environment of infinite revision, nothing feels fully committed.This became evident to me during a three-month design internship. Within minutes of arriving, I was assigned a laptop and access to multiple design applications. Tasks were time-bound, and efficiency was expected. Speed and continuous refinement were rooted in the studio’s expectations. What appeared as professional flexibility revealed itself as a temporal structure shaping how I made decisions.I see reversibility as a strength of digital design. Yet I have started to question how constant adjustment shapes my internal experience while designing. My process often includes micro-corrections: shifting kerning back and forth, testing multiple typefaces, printing and comparing iterations side by side. These actions are efficient, but create a subtle pressure to continuously optimize. Control becomes tied to my ability to revise. When a design feels unresolved, I do not respond by pausing, but by adjusting again. This has led me to not only examine my visual outcomes, but my relationship to control, finality, and correction. What happens when the possibility of undo is removed? And how does this affect my nervous system? This essay asks: How do irreversible typographic processes reshape my relationship to control, anxiety, and decision-making within contemporary graphic design? I hypothesize that removing undo and infinite revision will make my design decisions more deliberate while exposing how deeply my sense of control is conditioned by digital environments. Rather than arguing for analog production or rejecting digital tools, this essay uses analog environments to reveal the habits and expectations that digital systems have normalised within me. To test this hypothesis, I replaced the laptop with a typewriter and letterpress printing, both processes in which mistakes cannot be instantly corrected. I used my laptop to create a ‘baseline’ design to compare the rest of the analog experiments with. The text I used is the same for each medium, an excerpt from Machiel Spaan’s The Wandering Maker. The text discusses speed in today’s society.

"We live in world obsessed with speed. Speed is a quality: good, cheap, and nice and easy: fast food, speed dating, pitching for grants... the quicker the better. We generally regard slowness as a negative quality. If you look up synonyms for slow, you will find: hesitant, indecisive, lethargic, sluggish, lingering. All these synonyms refer to a supposed lack of speed in taking action"

I documented these sessions in detail: the time taken for decisions, the number of mistakes, my attention, my mental state, and general observations. For example, it took me a total of 4:33 minutes to typeset the block of text on my laptop, but while using the letterpress, it took me a total of 5 hours (spread over two days) to reproduce this same print. Rather than analysing finished outcomes alone, I began observing the tempo of decision-making: when I hesitated, when I rushed, and when I accepted imperfection. Additionally, I reflected on my own nervous system and how these conditions were linked to one another. The experiments were not attempts to produce a more authentic design, but methods for exposing how digital reversibility has shaped my expectations of control and optimisation.

The Typewriter

The typewriter introduced a different kind of limitation. It produced uniform letters but lacked the undo function. Mistakes were disruptive because correction would mean either starting again on a new page or accepting imperfection. This altered my sense of decision-making. On a laptop, decisions remain temporary; text can be reshaped after appearing. On the typewriter, decisions had to be thought out before being executed. I began by typing the text at the top left of the page, as I did with my laptop design, assuming it would be straightforward to produce a simple block of text. I was mistaken. The margins I had set caused words to be cut off abruptly, disrupting legibility. Unlike working digitally, the machine did not automatically move words to the next line. This forced me to become aware of spatial limits. After completing the text once, I attempted a second version, believing that familiarity with the line breaks would improve the result. Instead, the second version was worse.I realised that this medium required planning its physical composition on the page. Control shifted from correction to anticipation. Although the format resembled digital writing: a rectangular page, linear text, it lacked frictionless correction. This exposed how strongly digital systems exist within my habits.

When the machine began jamming on one side of the page, my immediate reaction was to restart it. This reflex revealed how deeply digital habits structure my expectations. Even in an analog setting, I automatically searched for a technical reset. Later on, instead of fixing the jam, I incorporated it into the composition by allowing text to overlap that area. The limitation shifted from an obstacle to an element in design or just acceptance.

Franco Berardi describes cognitive acceleration as a condition in which efficiency and optimisation become internalised behavioural patterns rather than technical features (Berardi, 2013). The typewriter slowed production physically, but the impulse to refine and optimize persisted internally. Removing undo did not eliminate my desire for control; it made it visible. I started to notice how much reassurance I get from the possibility of undo.
